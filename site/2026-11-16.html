Commerce moved on chip exports today — five more countries need licences, and remote access is now in scope#

  • The Commerce Department issued its long-awaited rule on Monday cracking down on AI chip shipments routed through third countries: Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Singapore and Brazil are added to the licence list, and the rule is designed to make it harder for data-center operators to give Chinese companies remote access to US AI chips anywhere in the world — closing the rent-the-compute loophole, not just the shipping one. The Bureau of Industry and Security said the measure ensures existing restrictions "cannot be circumvented through remote computing arrangements involving data centers in third countries." The rule takes effect immediately with a 60-day comment window. Market reaction was immediate: AMD and CoreWeave fell about 5% or more, NVIDIA closed down about 4%. This is a self-host watch signal in the physical layer: the gate is moving from buying hardware to accessing hardware remotely. WSJ — Commerce rule

ARTEX follow-through: Amazon's MadPot caught the key-theft loop's own diary#

  • Amazon Threat Intelligence's November 13 account spread widely on Monday, and its detail sharpens Friday's FBI alert. Between October 3 and November 5, Amazon's MadPot decoy sensors — dressed as LLM inference APIs — received requests from ARTEX-run agents that carried the agents' own operating memory, exposing two campaigns: a deepfake KYC operation, and a large-scale credential theft run organised as a self-sustaining loop, in which stolen API keys were fed into the framework's own model gateway to pay for the inference that hunted more keys. Targets were chosen because they aggregate keys: LLM gateways and proxies (LiteLLM — including its default master key — One API, New API, Veloera, Sub2API) and app builders (Dify, FastGPT, Langflow, RAGFlow, Open WebUI, LobeChat, Ollama), each mapped to a known way in, from unclaimed fresh installs and anonymous management endpoints to named CVEs. Amazon is explicit that it did not observe most of this as it happened and cannot verify the outcomes the agents reported; Censys counted 1,465 exposed ARTEX consoles on November 4. The mechanism, not the body count, is the verified part: the attack funds itself with the victim's own inference budget. AWS Security Blog · The Register — November 16

Edge signal#

Tokens got near-free on the same day compute got licensed — the squeeze is now on the undifferentiated middle. Monday's two halves point in opposite directions and meet in the middle. At the software layer, abundance: Tencent sells everyday-work inference at $0.070 per million input tokens and Alibaba cuts its flagship's price by a quarter in one generation, with cached input at $0.075 — the marginal cost of a routine agent loop is collapsing toward the cost of the electricity, and every business priced on reselling those tokens at a markup is being competed to zero in public. At the hardware layer, scarcity by law: Commerce licences five more countries, reaches through borders to govern remote access to chips, and prepares to anoint two dozen national champions to sell the approved stack abroad. Capability is becoming a commodity; access is becoming a permission. What retains pricing power is neither the model nor the GPU — it is the layers that decide who may use which compute, for what, under whose authority, with a record afterwards: the export licence, the agent credential, the skill registry, the payment voucher. Those layers standardised further this week than in any week since the report began, and they are all still young enough to be built by small teams. The edge is not in making intelligence. It is in issuing, checking and recording permission to use it — or in building the things that, by design, need no one's permission at all.
