The Riemann proof is certified: the Lean and Mathlib communities issued a joint statement today#

  • The joint statement, endorsed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, reads: "We have full confidence that Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct… We hereby certify that both the statement and the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis are correct," with all four of the community's pre-registered criteria satisfied — the statement is Mathlib's own vetted statement, the library compiles cleanly for dozens of independent builders, two independent comparators confirm the proved theorem is exactly that statement, and the exported proof has been accepted by several checkers, including con-leche and con-ron, which carry their own formal proofs that they cannot accept a false proof. MIT's Andrew Sutherland updated his verification page at 03:50 UTC today: all 175,187 modules compile, every module has been replayed through the Lean kernel, nanoda and con-leche accept the full export, both compare modes justify all five headline theorems, and lean4lean accepted all 175,187 compiled modules late Saturday. Two long single-threaded runs remain open — the end-to-end `lake comparator` and the Lean default/paranoid kernel replay of the export — and the Clay Mathematics Institute's prize process is a separate, human track that still lists the problem as open. A PhilArchive critique questioning whether a geometry predicate on the dependency path is vacuous remains unaddressed by the certification. The shape of the event is the story from Friday, completed: a 17-million-line machine-generated proof became certified knowledge in 48 hours because the artifact was checkable end-to-end by machines, while comprehension of it has still not begun. Banking watch: five banks in five weeks — and the standard-builders and prosecutors arrive#

  • The liability wave completed its first arc this week. Citi's new Section H treats anything a third-party AI agent does with a customer's credentials as authorized by the customer — including after those credentials are "further shared, leaked, or otherwise distributed" — and states the bank has no duty to investigate; Citi's own AI tools are carved out. PNC's rewritten Digital Services Agreement, published November 18 and effective December 19, forbids handing an agent a password at all. Citizens warned customers by email the same week. Agent money rails: AWS put x402 on the invoice — no wallet, no API key, per-task budgets#

  • Announced November 19 and still spreading: AWS Marketplace products can now be bought by agents over the x402 protocol through Amazon Bedrock AgentCore payments, charged per request in US dollars on the customer's existing AWS bill — no stablecoin wallet and no per-seller API key. The flow is enterprise-shaped: the seller answers with a signed price quote, AgentCore payments checks it against a per-task session budget and issues a payment token, and the charge lands with the same billing, budgets and controls finance teams already use. AWS's own worked example has a research agent comparing two listings and paying Tavily one cent for a single web search. Sellers register, integrate the AWS Marketplace x402 SDK, and get paid through normal Marketplace disbursements. The Register's framing is the honest tension: AWS is welcoming bot commerce while amazon.com bans bots. The significance for the rail wars is that the largest cloud billing relationship in the world now settles agent purchases in fiat by default — the crypto rail and the invoice rail are now competing for the same per-request transaction. Edge signal#

Know-your-agent arrived from three directions in one week — identity for agents is the next regulated layer, and it is unbuilt. A proof became knowledge this weekend because machines could certify it. In the same days, five banks finished declaring that an agent acting with a customer's credentials is the customer — certification by contract, because no technical certification exists. Singapore's central bank responded by announcing it will build the missing thing: a know-your-agent protocol, an identity and permission layer for software actors. AWS built its commercial version simultaneously — KYC-verified sellers, per-task budgets, signed quotes, audit trails — because enterprise agent commerce cannot run on anonymous endpoints. Four institutions, four vocabularies, one conclusion: the agent stack's scarce layer is no longer capability, which the Riemann artifact and a $125 Nvidia both say is abundant, but attested identity and scoped authorization for agents — who the agent is, whose authority it carries, what it may spend, and what proof survives the transaction. That layer is being designed right now, in public, by regulators and platforms, and almost nobody is building for the small end of it: scoped, revocable, auditable agent credentials for ordinary people and small operators. The builders who ship the small end of the identity layer — before the standard hardens around the large end — own the toll booth on every agent transaction that follows.
