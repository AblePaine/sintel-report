A canary study caught prompts being read days later — OpenRouter stripped the Zero Data Retention label from MiniMax endpoints#

  • OpenRouter emailed affected customers — no public post, no incident page — that prompts sent to MiniMax models "may have been read": a researcher planted unique test URLs in prompts from July to October, and a third party opened them 40 to 130 hours later, whether MiniMax served the request itself or a provider forwarded it. OpenRouter names AtlasCloud, Novita and StreamLake as forwarders, says the issue "sits with MiniMax and the providers that forwarded to it," relays MiniMax's explanation that content moderation was enabled on its endpoints before October 22 and that Zero Data Retention has since been enabled, and says it has asked MiniMax for its own account and not received one. One recipient's notice covered requests running to November 12, past the October 22 date in that explanation, and the advice in the notices is to rotate any secrets the prompts contained. The details match research published November 19 by HD Moore of Dragos: roughly two million bait prompts carrying unique links and fake credentials sent across AI services over five months, finding at least three hosting providers breached without knowing it, marketplace resellers logging prompts, and ZDR-flagged requests among those accessed. A privacy label on a routed endpoint was a claim; a canary is a measurement, and the measurement won. Privacy Affairs — what OpenRouter told customers · Progression — the two-million-canary study

The Riemann claim, one day on: machine checks largely pass, human understanding has not started#

  • Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge announced November 20 that, working with Claude over about six weeks, he had produced a proof of the Riemann hypothesis and its generalization — released not as a readable paper (the full written argument is said to run past 250,000 pages) but as Lean code: roughly 17.4 million lines across 175,187 files. By Saturday afternoon, MIT's Andrew Sutherland reported all modules compiling with only the three standard axioms, and two independently written checkers — nanoda and con-leche — accepting the full export, with con-leche's compare mode accepting all five headline theorems against the challenge statement. The official lake comparator run end-to-end and the Lean kernel replays were still running at the last update. Scientific American's updated headline says independent mathematicians are certain; the checkers' own pages are narrower — they verify that the Lean artifact proves Mathlib's statement of the hypothesis, not that anyone yet understands the argument, and the Clay Mathematics Institute still lists the problem as unsolved for its prize process. Both things are true at once, and the gap between them is the story: a frontier result whose verification is a compute job and whose comprehension is a years-long human one. The pattern is one pattern: every claim in the agent stack — privacy, capability, solvency, proof — is being re-based from assertion to verification, in public, at speed. Capability is abundant and getting cheaper by the week; Nvidia's tape and Inkling's laptop footprint say so from opposite ends. What is scarce, unowned, and repricing upward everywhere it appears is demonstrated trust: canaries, comparators, audit logs, revocation receipts, paid-verified registries. The builders who sell the measuring instruments will collect from every side of every one of these fights.
