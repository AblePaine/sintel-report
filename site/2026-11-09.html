Anthropic ships Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 — one model, two access tiers#

  • Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2 on November 9: the same underlying model split by safeguards. Fable 5.2 is generally available across Claude paid plans, Claude Code, the API and the major clouds at $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output, down from $10/$50, with output up to 30% faster than the 5.1 versions — all performance figures are Anthropic-reported. Mythos 5.2 drops most cyber and biology blocks and is limited to organizations vetted through Anthropic's Cyber and Life Sciences Verification Programs, or Claude Enterprise customers through Claude Security. Artificial Analysis put Fable 5.2 at the top of its Intelligence Index v5 at Max effort — and also the most expensive model in its comparison, at $26.54 per index task. The launch landed the same day Anthropic's pre-IPO quiet period ended, with formal marketing of the shares to investors reported to start Tuesday, November 10. Reuters · The Hype News

Meta releases Muse Ember: Haiku-class agent work at a third of the cost per task#

  • Meta released Muse Ember on November 9, the smallest model in the Muse family, built for the repetitive layer of agent work — tool calls, coding subtasks, routing, extraction, long-document parsing. Pricing is $0.10 per million input tokens, $0.004 cached input and $0.50 per million output, with a 1M-token context window and outputs up to 512K tokens. Meta says Ember matches Claude Haiku 5.5's best score on Artificial Analysis's Intelligence Index v5 at about a third of the cost per task — roughly $0.14 per task against $2.39 for Muse Spark 1.3 at a near-identical index score (36 vs 37). Ember is the first model trained using Muse Wonder Full, is available now via the Meta Model API, Muse Code and OpenRouter, and its weights stay private. The Decoder's read of Meta's own tables adds the caveat: Haiku still beats Ember on real coding tasks in FrontierCode 2.0-AA, 34.9% to 27.2%. The Decoder · Unite.AI · OpenRouter

Google adds a confidential-computing TPU and a doubled inference TPU#

  • Google expanded its eighth-generation TPU line on November 9 with TPU 8tx and TPU 8i Max — four TPU systems in one year, a first for Google. TPU 8tx pairs TPU 8t silicon with 5th-generation AMD EPYC hosts using SEV, pitched at regulated and government inference, private fine-tuning and secure agent execution, with data and weights protected while in use. TPU 8i Max puts two TPU 8i dies in one package for double the compute, SRAM and HBM bandwidth, aimed at many-agent, long-context work. Google also said TPUs now have nearly 100 customers. The gaps matter as much as the claims: no price, region or availability date was published for either system (Google's Amin Vahdat told Bloomberg they arrive next year), and no attestation design or independent benchmark accompanied the confidential-computing claim — the security boundary is described in fragments across the Google and AMD posts, not in a reviewable document. Banking watch: the agent blocks go transatlantic — still no aggregator breach#

  • The Telegraph reported on November 8 that five UK lenders — HSBC, Nationwide, NatWest, Barclays and Lloyds — have moved to block AI agents from customer accounts, in some cases including transactions made without the customer's direct involvement. The UK moves are described as pre-emptive: Lloyds' CFO said the bank had seen no evidence of agents accessing accounts. Whether a formal published prohibition exists is unconfirmed — the report quotes caution from finance chiefs rather than clause text, and no bank terms were located in follow-up coverage. The US position is unchanged from October: JPMorgan Chase, Charles Schwab and Citigroup block third-party agents logging in with customer credentials while permitting read-only data through connectors such as Plaid, and Wells Fargo's amended terms take effect November 24 — fifteen days out — assigning loss to the customer who delegates access. No financial aggregator breach, crack or outage was verified in this window, and no delegated-authorization standard launched. Jaroha analysis · Hunton

MCP gateways become a product category: Appwrite's MCP1 opens to all teams#

  • Appwrite opened MCP1, its hosted MCP gateway, to every team: one URL fronting every MCP server a team runs, with OAuth 2.1 sign-in, per-group tool rules (including name-pattern rules and "ask first" rules that require human confirmation via MCP elicitation), credentials sealed inside the gateway, and a full audit log of every call. The catalog lists 154 remote MCP servers; pricing is free for individuals, $12 per member/month for teams and $29 for enterprise. The launch post is dated November 3 and the product is now generally offered. Appwrite's own lessons from running it internally are the telling part: read-only tokens "do most of the safety work," oversized tool answers are cut at 1 MB by default because big answers make agents worse, and agents are pointed at saved, team-agreed query definitions instead of writing their own. Coinbase ships Wallet MCP: agents prepare, humans approve#

  • Coinbase Developer Platform published Wallet MCP documentation on November 9, connecting AI assistants to Coinbase Wallet across Base, Ethereum, Arbitrum, Optimism, Polygon, BSC and Avalanche. Agents can read balances and history and prepare DeFi transactions, but every write — transfers, swaps, contract calls — requires manual approval in the wallet. The integration natively supports x402 USDC micropayments on Base, gated by a user-set spending cap before any payment completes. It is the sanctioned-shape counterpoint to the bank blocks in the same news cycle: capability behind explicit per-action approval and a hard cap, rather than a credential handed over. BasisDesk

Sovereign-AI lens, continuing#

  • The sovereignty argument acquired a hardware SKU and a services SKU in the same day. Google now sells confidential inference as a named TPU tier — data sealed even from the cloud operator, with the attestation details still unpublished — and Toptal now sells "full data ownership" as a professional-services package built on other vendors' models and clouds. In both cases sovereignty is the marketing word and a rented layer is the product. The counter-evidence in the same cycle is structural: banks on two continents are blocking outside agents outright rather than renting a trust layer, and the one money integration that shipped today, Coinbase's Wallet MCP, keeps the human's approval on every write. The market is splitting between sovereignty-as-a-service and sovereignty-as-architecture, and only one of those compounds in the owner's favor.

Edge signal#

Capability got cheaper today; permission got scarcer. In a single cycle, Meta cut the price of competent agent grunt work to about $0.14 a task, Anthropic put its strongest model behind a two-tier access split, Google sold confidentiality as a hardware tier without publishing how to verify it, and banks on a second continent moved to keep agents out of customer accounts entirely. The scarce asset in the agent economy is no longer intelligence — it is authorized access: who may act, on what, under whose cap, with what proof afterwards. The gateway, the audit log, the approval prompt and the revocation switch are where the money and the liability now sit, and every one of them is a layer a local, owner-controlled stack gets by architecture rather than by subscription. Builds that hold their own permissions — offline where possible, capped and logged where connected — are positioned on the side of that split that compounds.
