OpenAI priced autonomous vulnerability discovery: 948 kernel root exploits for $1.2M#

  • OpenAI's security post "A thousand fewer paths to root" (published November 4) reports that an autonomous pipeline built on an unreleased cyber model audited about 7% of the Linux kernel in a mid-October run and came back with 948 unique local privilege-escalation vulnerabilities, each with a working exploit and a patch. Scouts picked 5,034 audit targets; analysts returned 2,308 candidates; dynamic validation confirmed 1,724; the total API bill was $1,213,430 — about $1,279 per validated, patched bug. A July run of the same harness on GPT-5.6 Sol found 166, so the count rose more than fivefold in three months despite intervening kernel hardening. The agents also produced at least 10 exploitation techniques outside experts said they had not seen; details are withheld. Figures are OpenAI-reported and not independently reproduced, and human reviewers still check every exploit and patch before disclosure — within 10 days, 61 of the bugs were patched upstream and 43 had patches in staging. OpenAI's own conclusion: a low-privileged account on an unpatched Linux box should be treated as functionally equivalent to root for a well-resourced actor. SecurityWeek · Help Net Security

OpenAI answers the ballot box: a community compact, and a request to make it law#

  • Two days after data-center restrictions swept local ballots, OpenAI published a National Community Compact and a Compute Campus Policy Blueprint (November 5). For campuses it owns and operates, it commits to paying the full cost of its electricity and the grid infrastructure to serve it, posting financial assurances before major grid spending, publishing site-level water and power data, commissioning independent noise studies, and ending NDAs with public officials. The blueprint then asks lawmakers to make the same rules binding on everyone: large-load tariffs at state utility commissions, mandatory disclosure, clawbacks on tax breaks, faster transmission permitting. The limits are stated in the documents themselves: the compact covers only OpenAI-operated campuses, names no penalties, and specifies no independent verification. A lab volunteering for rules it can already meet, and asking that they be made compulsory, is the incumbent play — compliance as a moat. Decrypt · OpenAI

China blocks SoftBank's $5.375B ABB Robotics deal — over robot data#

  • ABB confirmed November 5 that China's foreign-investment security review has prohibited SoftBank's acquisition of the ABB Robotics business in China. China is the division's biggest market, about 30% of its robot business, and hosts its global headquarters in Shanghai. The deal is not terminated — both companies say they are working on it — but the structure, timing, and price are all now uncertain, and SoftBank fell about 7% in Tokyo. Per Financial Times reporting via Reuters, the stated concern was data: robotics operating data leaving the country and feeding foreign AI training, including at OpenAI, in which SoftBank is a major investor. It is the second major AI deal Beijing has blocked this year, after ordering Meta to unwind Manus. The pattern is now a rule of the robotics map: physical-AI assets with China-heavy operations and exportable data are not buyable by foreign capital. Reuters via Duke FM · PYMNTS

A 65B open model claims the US coding lead — for $32M of R&D#

  • Ricursive Intelligence released Ripple-65B Preview on November 4: a dense 65B model, trained from scratch on 12T tokens, Apache 2.0, scoring a self-reported 80.5 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 — top among US open weights on that table, ahead of models up to 15× its size — and 79.1 on SWE-bench Pro v2.0 Hard. The full R&D cost, by the company's account, was $32M. Three checkpoints ship, including an experimental NVFP4 build aimed at DGX Spark-class hardware. The asterisks matter: the comparison table mixes harnesses, Ripple trails badly on tool-calling (MCP Atlas 71.7 vs. Muse Spark Open's 89.2) and science benchmarks, and on the independent Terminal-Bench 4.0 board the open leader is Kimi K3.1 Pro at 63.64% — Ripple is not yet listed there. Even discounted, the shape is the signal: near-frontier agentic coding at desktop-adjacent scale, under a clean Apache license, built by a five-person team for the price of a mid-size funding round. SpaceX's $40B GPU lease hits the market — and investors carry the residual risk#

  • Goldman Sachs and Apollo have begun selling slices of SpaceX's roughly $40B GPU lease financing ahead of formal syndication, with some investors asked for up to $500M each, Bloomberg reported November 4. The package (announced October 26) has an Apollo-managed vehicle buying Nvidia Vera Rubin hardware and leasing it to SpaceX over roughly five-year terms; SpaceX owes about $40B in principal plus interest, must keep paying rent even if the equipment is damaged, and gives no guarantee of what the chips will be worth when the leases end — that residual risk sits with the buyers. Together with a $7B lease prepayment it covers about $47.3B of equipment due in the first half of 2027. SpaceX shares fell nearly 4% on the news. The AI buildout's financing is now a syndicated product with the depreciation risk sliced out to asset managers: when the sell-down of a deal this size is the news, the question to watch is the pricing, not the headline. Decompile / clean-room watch — the jailbreak lane is now agent-driven#

  • The Switch 2 jailbreak story reached mainstream coverage today (How-To Geek, November 5): researcher Sammy Hajhamid (pepsipu) built ns2jb, a tethered, software-only Switch 2 jailbreak, with OpenAI's Codex wired directly to the hardware — issuing controller inputs, watching the console's output, and rebooting it after crashes while hunting the flaw, which landed in the backward-compatible SmileBASIC 4. The OCC has issued its first examiner framework for bank AI use, placing a fully autonomous customer-facing payment agent in the highest risk tier regardless of test performance, and naming identity, provenance, delegation, and revocation as the things examiners should probe — guidance, not a delegated-authorization standard, so no callout fires. Edge signal of the day#

  • The buildout's scarce input is no longer compute, data, or even power — it is verifiable control, and every front repriced it today. China blocked a $5.4B robotics deal not over hardware but over who would control the data the hardware generates. OpenAI spent $1.2M proving a machine can find root exploits cheaper than any human market — and kept the techniques secret, because control of the method is the asset. Its compact asks lawmakers to convert yesterday's voter revolt into rules, because controlling the terms of permission is worth more than any single campus. A regulator wrote identity, provenance, delegation, and revocation into an examiner framework while two vendors launched products to sell exactly those answers. And the open-weight frontier kept arriving in shapes — Apache-clean at 65B, toll-gated at 2.8T — whose common feature is that the operator, not the vendor, holds the controls. The positions that compound are the ones where control is structural rather than promised: owned weights on owned metal, data that never leaves the building, software that asks no platform for permission to serve its owner. The market spent today paying premiums for approximations of that shape. The portfolio already holds the original.
