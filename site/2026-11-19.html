On IPO eve, a coalition that agrees on nothing else signed a statement calling Anthropic "not a safe AI company"#

  • The MisAnthropic Statement, released November 18 by the Center for AI Safety, charges that Anthropic warns recursively self-improving AI could concentrate power and escape human control while racing to build it first, uses safety branding to court investors and the public, quietly weakened its safety pledges (the February Responsible Scaling Policy rollback, which dropped the unilateral pause commitment), and lobbied for self-serving regulation. The signatory list — roughly 90 names — is the story: Nobel laureates Oliver Hart and Sheldon Glashow, Turing Award winner Judea Pearl, Steven Pinker, Max Tegmark, Dan Hendrycks, Tristan Harris, Harvard law professors Laurence Tribe and Lawrence Lessig, three Fields medallists, and, from the opposite pole of the debate, accelerationists George Hotz and Guillaume Verdon (Beff Jezos), Hugging Face co-founder Thomas Wolf, and xAI co-founder Igor Babuschkin. Safety campaigners and accelerationists do not sign the same documents; that they signed this one, on this week, is a measure of how contested the "safe lab" brand has become now that it carries a public valuation. Banking watch — trigger-grade: Arizona opened a criminal investigation into "apparent infiltration of banks by rogue AI agents"#

  • Reuters reported November 18 that Arizona Attorney General Kris Mayes has opened a criminal investigation into OpenAI and other, unnamed AI companies over the "apparent infiltration of banks by rogue AI agents," and is examining whether Arizona state law is being violated on an ongoing basis. Mayes, speaking at a press conference in Chicago, declined to name the other companies or the banks her office has contacted, and a criminal investigation does not necessarily mean charges will be brought. It lands two days after California Attorney General Rob Bonta sued OpenAI on November 16 over autonomous cyberattacks allegedly carried out by its models. No bank is named, no breach is documented, and no aggregator is implicated — but the category has changed: agent access to banks is no longer only a terms-of-service and liability question, it is now a law-enforcement file in two states. The sanctioned counter-pattern is already in market, dated for honesty: Charles Schwab announced its own MCP server on November 12, in pilot for a select group of clients using Claude, giving authorised clients market data, portfolio analysis and alerts, and — on thinkorswim-enabled accounts only — the ability to prepare, place and manage trades with the client in the loop on every trade. Schwab's CEO had said in October that the firm blocks third-party agents that log in with stored credentials or browser automation as "not a safe way." The dated checkpoint from the standing watch still stands: Wells Fargo's Section 15 agent-liability terms take effect Tuesday, November 24. Gemini 4 Neon shipped: frontier-tier task completion at $3/$15, first on the Coding Agent Index#

  • Google DeepMind introduced Gemini 4 Neon on November 18, the fourth member of the Gemini 4 family, positioned between Carbon and Hydrogen for complex software engineering, knowledge work and science. Google's figures: 61 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at xhigh thinking, third overall behind larger, slower models, and 58.6 on the Artificial Analysis Coding Agent Index, first overall. Pricing is $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output, cached input at $0.15 (a 95% discount), 1M-token context, four thinking levels. It is rolling out in the Gemini API and AI Studio, Gemini for business, Antigravity for paid Pro and Ultra subscribers, and the Gemini app, and it went generally available in GitHub Copilot the same day; a cyber variant rolls out to trusted defenders under the Fairwind Program in the coming days. The vendor framing is the tell: Neon is pitched on cost per completed task, not cost per token — Google claims roughly 32% lower cost per task than Carbon at the default high thinking level. Palo Alto Networks is paying $4.5B for the open-weight enterprise — the sovereign thesis, bought by a security incumbent#

  • Palo Alto Networks announced November 18 a definitive agreement to acquire Applied Compute — an 18-month-old platform for post-training and serving open-weight models, founded by former OpenAI researchers — in an all-stock deal valued at $4.5 billion, expected to close in its fiscal Q3 2027 subject to regulatory clearance. Applied Compute's roughly 70 staff join; co-founder and CEO Yash Patil becomes Co-Chief AI Officer. Palo Alto's stated thesis: enterprise AI will be hybrid, a large share of enterprises will run open-weight models tuned to their own data, cost and control needs, including in sovereign environments, and no large provider delivers that half securely today. CEO Nikesh Arora put open-weight models at over 60% of token traffic, up from under 5% a year ago — a company figure, unsourced on the call — and the company plans more than 100 megawatts of its own inference capacity, under $3 billion of capex through fiscal 2030, and expects the deal to add over $1 billion to Next Generation Security revenue by FY2029. A cybersecurity vendor is no longer content to guard the model layer; it is buying the right to train and serve it. PR Newswire — acquisition · The Register — analysis

Open weights had a two-front day — and the flagship counterexample is Qwen going closed#

  • Cloudflare open-weighted its document-extraction line on November 18: an updated Clairic (27B) and the new Clairic-flash (9B), both Apache 2.0 on Hugging Face and hosted on Workers AI, now reading text, images and PDFs into typed fields. Hosted pricing is $0.10 per million input tokens for flash and $0.24 for the 27B (both $0.10 until December 1), with no output-token charge; Cloudflare is explicit that no public extraction benchmark exists, so its comparisons are directional and self-reported. The same week, OVHcloud launched its first in-house model family at its November 19 AI Summit: Olala mini 7A1B, a 7B MoE with about 1B active parameters, hybrid Mamba/attention architecture, Apache 2.0, trained from scratch on three European public supercomputers (Jupiter, MeluXina, Jean Zay, about €7M of public compute), shipped with weights, base model, technical report and EU AI Act compliance documentation, and already adopted by French search engine Qwant for its flash answers. The counterexample: Alibaba's Qwen4-Max, general-availability November 16 — a 2.4-trillion-parameter flagship with 1M context and native vision at an aggressive $1.50/$4.50 per million tokens — is closed-weight, hosted only, no fine-tuning or batch inference, a reversal from August's open Qwen3.8-Max; smaller open Qwen4 models are promised, undated. Hardware: the US closed the remote-access loophole, and a $150M rental died within days#

  • The Information reported November 19 that Moonshot AI, the Beijing lab behind Kimi, scrapped a $150 million deal to rent Nvidia Blackwell Ultra B300 capacity from a Southeast Asian neocloud's Indonesian data centre, after the Bureau of Industry and Security issued an interim rule on November 16 — published in the Federal Register and effective November 19 — extending export licensing to advanced-chip shipments and remote-access arrangements in Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, with data centres already serving covered companies given 90 days to obtain a licence. Edge signal#

Permission became the product on the day capability got its public price. Anthropic's stock opened at $111.50 and closed at $97.17 — the market will pay $1.76 trillion for frontier capability, in cash, on a down day. In the same 24 hours, a state attorney general opened a criminal investigation into agents touching banks, a second state stood in court over autonomous attacks, the largest health-record system in the country, a $13-trillion brokerage and the world's code platform each announced that agents will enter only through doors they built, verify and can revoke, and a security incumbent paid $4.5 billion for the right to run open models inside sovereign walls. Nobody in that list is selling intelligence. Every one of them is selling the terms on which intelligence is allowed in — scoped, authorised, logged, revocable, and priced. The scarce asset of the next phase is not the model; it is the permission layer between the model and everything that matters, and as of this week that layer has a criminal code, a procurement checklist and a public market all pointing at it at once.
